# 2026-07-29 — M10: brush selection

Children 1+ of a tool node are selection brushes. They were parsed and collected
from the start and then thrown away; now they narrow what gets built to the
stretches of crease they cover, and the bead ends on a flat cap square to the
crease at whatever physical point the brush's surface crosses it.

## What landed

`FilletBrush.{h,cc}` — a `BrushVolume`: the brushes' triangles, an AABB tree over
them, and the two questions clipping a spine asks. `chainSelection` in
`fillet::detail` turns those answers into parameter intervals along a chain, and
`Chain::keep` carries them. `appendChainCells` honours them for every tool;
`GeometryEvaluator` unions children 1+ and hands the result to `buildFilletTool`.

Seven new unit tests, and `case_brush_halfchain` — written at M1 against a hand
reference and red ever since — is green and off the expectations ledger. The
promoted regression case grows a fourth column so the cap has a committed
baseline.

## Intersect the spine, not the volume

The clip is a parameter along a segment, and the two cross-sections are
interpolated to it. That is exact rather than approximate, and it is worth
saying why: hulling two consecutive sections **is** the linear interpolation of
the cross-section between them, so slicing that cell at a parameter and building
a cell to the section at that parameter give the same solid. Nothing is
resampled and nothing is approximated by the choice.

Three things follow that clipping the finished tool would not give:

- **The termination is a physical point.** Changing `$fn` on the target moves its
  vertices; it does not move where the brush's surface crosses the crease.
- **A brush boundary landing on a station is a parameter near 0 or 1**, not a
  decision about whether that station is in. There is nothing to flip.
- **The cap is the full cross-section**, square to the spine. Shaving the bead
  lengthwise is not something this construction can express.

## The brush's own angle must not reach the result

A brush selects a *point along an edge*. Nothing about the orientation of the
brush's surface where it crosses the spine may show up in the cap, because the
cap's plane comes from the crease — it is the plane the two wall normals span,
which on a straight crease is square to the spine. That is true by construction
here, since the brush contributes one scalar and nothing else, but "true by
construction" is how the wrong thing gets shipped.

So it is pinned. A half-space brush whose plane is tilted 20° and 35° away from
the spine, positioned to pass through a point *on* the crease, is checked to give
the same solid as cutting the bead with the plane **perpendicular** to the crease
at that point — and explicitly not the same solid as cutting it with the brush.
The two differ by two thirds of a percent of the bead, and by 0.35 mm of reach:
the square cap stops the whole bead at `y = 4`, the brush surface runs on to 4.35
on the far side of it. Every other brush test uses an axis-aligned box, where
those two answers coincide and the case proves nothing.

## Do not clip U, and the test that says so

`W` is clipped; the canal `U` runs a segment past each end of every kept
stretch. Shortening it to match would let its round cap bulge back through the
cut plane and scoop a dish out of the flat end face. Overhanging is free — past
where `W` stops there is nothing for `U` to subtract from.

The unit test states the whole claim as one equality: the clipped tool and
`unclipped ∩ brush` agree, both ways round, to a millionth of the volume. Each
direction fails differently — material the brush should have kept is the scoop,
material past the brush is a bead that did not stop — so both are checked.

## Crossings carry a direction, so there is no parity to get wrong

The first instinct is to count crossings and alternate. It is worse than it
looks: one crossing miscounted inverts every interval after it, and every chain
needs a global inside/outside test to start from.

The brush is a solid with consistent outward normals, so the sign of the ray
against the triangle it hits says whether that passage goes in or out. The state
before the first crossing is then read off the first crossing. Only a chain that
crosses nothing at all needs a point tested, and that test is the same trick
rather than a count: shoot a ray, take the nearest face, and ask which way it
faces — a ray that leaves through a back face started inside. Three directions
vote, so a ray that happens to leave through an edge, where the nearest face's
orientation says nothing, is outvoted rather than believed.

A segment through a shared edge meets both triangles that own it, at the same
parameter and the same direction: one passage reported twice. Those collapse. A
pair at the same parameter with *opposite* directions is a genuine graze, in and
straight back out, and is left for the minimum-length guard to discard as a
zero-length interval.

## Where the selection lives, and the bookkeeping that costs

The intervals are cut in **chain-parameter space** — station `i` at parameter
`i` — because that is the space a brush boundary is a fixed point in. The cells
are built in **section-index space**. Those are the same space right up until a
chain meets a junction, and then they are not: truncation slides an end section
back off its station, and a runout replaces one station with a fan of seven.

The first attempt papered over this by requiring the selection to cover the whole
segment beside a junction before the junction would be built. It is wrong on the
commonest model there is. A cube's edge is a single segment, so *any* partial
clip anywhere along it de-anchors both of its ends, and a brush over one corner
of a cube would lose that corner while sitting entirely inside the brush.

What replaced it is to carry, per chain, where each section actually sits along
it — `sectionAt`, the identity until something moves it — and to map the
selection through its piecewise-linear inverse once, just before the cells are
built. Truncation and runout each write one entry. The requirement on a junction
drops to what it should always have been: **every chain meeting there has to
reach the vertex**, and nothing is asked about how far past it the selection
runs.

## Two rules that are trades, not deductions

- **A junction needs every incident crease to reach it.** A corner cell closing
  three beads when only two arrive is a lump sitting on the model, not a corner.
  Where a brush takes some of the creases at a vertex and not the others, the
  ones it takes are capped flat like any other clip and no corner is built.
- **A corner cell may overshoot its brush.** If the selection reaches the vertex
  but stops short of where truncation cuts the spine back, the corner is still
  built and reaches a fraction of a segment past the brush. Brushes are selection
  volumes with slack designed in, and one that reaches a corner meant to take it,
  so the overshoot is the lesser wrong against losing the corner outright.

## What is not done here

**The size gate runs before the brush, on the whole chain.** A crease that cannot
carry the size is refused whether or not a brush picks out part of it, and the
warning fires for creases the user did not select. The gate's question is about
the crease and the model around it, and selecting half of one does not make the
size fit there — but a chain whose *selected* part has room while its far end
does not is refused today, and answering that properly means teaching the gate to
read `keep`, which is a change to `chainContacts` and not to selection.

**The empty-selection warning counts edges, not length.** It says how many of the
candidate edges the brush took any part of. That is the unit the model was
written in, and it is what tells a user their brush missed; it is not a measure
of how much bead they got.

**Grazing boundaries are documented, not solved.** A brush face nearly parallel
to the spine gives an ill-conditioned crossing parameter. It will not crash — the
minimum-length guard eats the sliver — but where the cut lands is not meaningful
at that angle. Cross edges cleanly.
