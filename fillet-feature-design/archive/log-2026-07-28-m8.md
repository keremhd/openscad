# 2026-07-28 — M8: junction cells

Chains used to run their rolling ball all the way to the vertex where they met,
and M7 kept the damage contained by subtracting each chain's canal from its own
wedge only. Both halves of that are now gone. A spine stops where its ball first
touches a wall it is not riding, a corner cell fills what the wedges no longer
reach, and one subtraction runs over everything.

## What landed

`Junction` and `chainJunctions` in `fillet::detail`, a `C` on `RoundSection` so a
section can be interpolated to a truncation point, and inside `buildRoundSolid`
the truncation, the corner cell and the corner balls. `buildWedgeSolid` is
untouched: overlapping wedges *are* what a chamfered corner is.

Six comparisons against exact references in `FilletCompare_test.cc`, all of them
new: the cube corner, a three- and a four-face apex, a three- and a four-face
pocket, the inside box corner, and the section a tenth of a radius above it.

## The truncation rule is the whole of it

§6.3 asks for a 3×3 solve for `P` and then a truncation at `P`. Written the
other way round — *stop where the ball first reaches any wall of the junction* —
the same rule needs no solve, no valence case, and no special handling when the
solve is rejected: the walls a spine rides sit at exactly `r` all along it and
never bind, so only a wall it is running into does. At degree three every
incident spine stops at the same point, which is what lets the corner close
exactly rather than be patched, and `P` stops being a construction and becomes
the name for that coincidence.

That framing is what makes the global subtraction safe. It is not that
truncation makes it merely tolerable: with the spines stopped, the canal is
exactly the set of positions the ball can occupy, so nothing in it is material
another crease still needs. The M7 probe test — a box in the top edge's bead,
outside that bead's own ball, inside the vertical edge's — passes unchanged, and
would go red the moment truncation broke.

## Degree four came along for free, and had to

The plan puts valence ≥ 4 in M9, and the intention here was to stop at three.
Truncating at a four-face apex without giving it a corner turned a passing case
into a crash, so the choice was between a special case that leaves the spines
long there and the general construction. The general one is smaller: solve every
triple of walls, keep the centres that clear the walls they were not solved
against, hull balls at what is left. Degree three is the single-triple case and
falls out of it. The symmetric four-sided apex is the one that says the
feasibility filter has not thrown the answer away — all four triples give the
same centre, and it is the right one.

Asymmetric was then tested rather than assumed. Shearing the pyramid moves its
apex off the axis, the offset planes stop sharing a point, and the ball gets
several extreme positions: two at four sides, three at five, four at six. All of
them match the exact rounded solid, both signs. So the enumeration and the filter
are general, and what is left of M9 is not about valence:

- **Mixed-sign vertices are unsafe.** The constraints come from the walls of the
  *selected* creases only, so where three concave creases meet a convex one the
  convex crease's far face constrains the ball and the solve never hears about
  it. `dir`, applied uniformly, is meaningless there too. Nothing in the suite
  has such a vertex yet.
- **A junction that gets no centre still gets its spines cut.** Truncation runs
  off the wall normals without asking whether a corner was built, so a rejected
  solve leaves a gap — under-fill on a fillet, an un-removed lump on a round —
  and no warning. That failure mode is new here, and the runout fallback is what
  it is waiting for.

Neither is about degree. Unequal radii remain out of scope.

## The reference that made the checking easy

Rounding a convex solid by `r` is exactly shrinking it by `r` and growing it
back with a ball of `r`, and both halves are computable: eroding a polytope is
pushing each face plane in, and growing a convex body by a ball is the hull of
balls at its vertices. `refRoundedConvex` reads both off the solid's own mesh, so
a case states its model once instead of restating its geometry as a reference,
and one comparison pins every edge and every corner at the same time. It is what
turned "the apex looks plausible" into a number.

The concave cases reuse it on the void: a pyramidal pocket is a pyramid the ball
rolls around the outside of.

## Three coincidences, and where they had to go

Every failure this milestone produced downstream was two surfaces resting on each
other instead of crossing. The tool already had one such trick — the pentagon's
`eps` past each wall — and three more turned out to be needed:

1. **The canal's last section is a great disc of the corner ball.** Stopping the
   spine exactly at `P` puts the two subtracted solids face to face over a whole
   disc. The spine runs a thousandth of a radius past instead. Without it, a
   `$fn = 4` cone's tool cannot be dilated by CGAL at all.
2. **The corner cell and the wedge it continues.** Handed the same section the
   wedge stops at, they abut on one shared face. The corner cell hulls against a
   section slightly further back, so they overlap in a thin slab.
3. **The corner cell's own wall face.** It must not sit at the pentagon's `eps`,
   or it lands in the neighbouring wedges' plane; it must not sit beyond it
   either, or the tool shows the caller's boolean two overshoot depths past one
   wall instead of one. Half of `eps` is inside both bounds, and the range around
   it behaves identically — it is a bracket, not a tuned constant.

## What is still red, and why

**A bare `round_tool()` solid cannot be converted to a Nef polyhedron.** Applying
it is fine — `difference()` against the model renders on either backend, and so
does every case in the suite — but the tool on its own, which
`round-tool-tests.scad` deliberately shows beside the applied result, forces the
conversion on the CGAL backend and trips an assertion. The cause is the same
family as everything above: at every corner a sphere sits tangent to three walls,
and the surfaces meeting along those tangencies leave triangles too small to
survive CGAL quantising its input. `render-cgal_round-tool-tests` and
`render-csg-cgal_round-tool-tests` are disabled with that reason written down;
the Manifold baselines carry the case, and the shape is pinned exactly in Catch2.
Reducing the tangential contact — the circular-segment section M7 backed out is
the obvious candidate — is what would fix it properly.

**The two apex cases fail their sandwich check, and correctly.** `sandwich` asks
that the result stay within `r` of the model. Rounding a sharp apex cannot: a
ball of 6 seated in a `$fn = 3` cone of 30 × 60 tops out 18.7 below the tip, so
the right answer is three radii from the model. The check has no reference to
compare against on these cases and this is the coarseness it was documented to
have; the exact comparison in Catch2 is what says the corner is right. The
expectation lines are updated to say that instead of blaming a missing corner.

**The two pocket cases still fail on dilation**, for the CGAL reason above.

## The picture

The cube's baseline is the whole milestone in one image: the corners were three
beads meeting in a facet and are now spherical. The junction cases read as a
contour stack — at a section below the corner the pocket is a circle, which is
the ball cut by a plane, and there is no other shape it could be.
